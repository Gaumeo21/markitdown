1. Understanding of a Production System

1.1 What is a Production System?

The term “production system” originates from the world of production management and describes the systematic approach to the order fulfillment process, i.e. the process of fulfilling customer orders from the date of the order set by the customer to the time of delivery of the manufactured products to the customer.

This approach describes the principles, processes, methods and rules of the order fulfillment process. It serves as a guide to show associates and managers how to carry out tasks and responsibilities.

Applicable processes, methods, and rules are never set in stone, but are further developed in the course of continuous improvement.

The sub-functions of this order fulfillment process are

- Order transmission

- Order processing, including scheduling

in the production and logistics system

- Manufacturing of products

- Packaging and dispatch

---

1.2 Why do Companies have a

Production System?

Along the path from mass production with few variants (at the time of Henry Ford) to far greater product diversity, faster and shorter development and product life cycles, the requirements placed on production have changed considerably. Today, industrial companies are faced with the challenge of securing and increasing the earnings potential of their companies in the long term while dealing with high rates of growth, high cost pressure and increasingly volatile markets. The ability to operate and optimize complex production and logistics networks in a stable fashion is an essential skill for the future viability of industrial companies. Procedures, methods, and rules are required to conserve resources, produce efficiently and eliminate waste.

Many companies have now developed their own production systems by describing the general principles and standards that

apply globally to the organization and the working and production methods within their company and serve to ensure continious process improvements.

The oldest and best known of these production systems is the Toyota Production System (TPS). Most current production systems, especially in the automotive industry sector, but also in other industries, build on the principles of the TPS and have been tailored individually and further developed.

Companies such as BMW, Daimler, Porsche and Ford, to mention just a few well-known car manufacturers, have thus been working with their own production systems for years. As different as the systems may be in terms of the details, they all have one thing in common: the aspiration to produce as efficiently, quickly, flexibly and leanly as possible in order to be capable of following the changing needs of customers in a cost-effective and competitive manner at all times.

1.3 History of Lean Production

The foundations of today’s lean production systems were laid in the beginnings of the Industrial Revolution in the 18th century and have been refined and further developed continuously ever since. In 1798 laid the groundwork

The “Reichsausschuss für Arbeitszeitermittlung (REFA)” was founded in Germany in 1924 by the “Verein Deutscher Ingenieure” (VDI = Association of German Engineers) in

order to systematically reappraise

2.4 Industrial Engineering as part of BPS

Industrial Engineering (IE) at Bosch includes the business fields “Design of work systems” and “Time management”.

The principles, methods and tools developed are supporting the Bosch Production System by the organizational optimization

of planning, design, performance and execution of human labor.

- Design of work systems

The aim of Design of work systems (ergonomics ) is to optimize the arrangement, with regard to time and space, of the working conditions and sequence as well as the working equipment and devices and thus create the basis for efficient and faultless work.

The working person is at the heart of this.

The work systems designed in accordance with the principles of BPS do not cause any health hazards and/or even damage for our associates. This is ensured by the application of the appropriate design principles for occupational work, the development and testing of methods and the use of tools such as IGEL or E rgoCheck .

---

Furthermore, we analyze the type, scope and conditions of the cooperation of our employees and the interface between employee and machine. The main aspects under consideration are the distribution and structuring of tasks such as multiple machine operation, quick changeover and cyclical material supply. - Time management

Time management refers to the method for determining, processing and u sage of work-based time data, i.e. the duration for certain work operations. This time data is an essential element and basis of company decision-making processes and is used for planning and control of production sequences and processes as well as for the calculation of orders and products.

At Bosch the preferred method of determining time data is the MTM system . Building on a good ergonomic workplace layout based on MTM findings, the optimal working methods can be achieved. The standard work time is derived from the working method. The introduction of the MTM system at Bosch was a paradigm shift in terms of time management. It enables rationalization progress and increased productivity, without associates having to raise their personal performance. In other words: increased output with the same effort.

---

Basic principles for IE activities and tasks include reliable ergonomic findings and IE-relevant rules in the form of laws, works agreements, staff rules, regulations, directives, etc. Observing the principle of legality is our top priority.

By means of central directives, in particular CD-04603 (Time Data in Routings), works agreements and/or board-ofmanagement letters, company-internal binding standards are defined and documented in the Bosch-Norms N62A (Work System Design) and N62C (Time Data).

The necessary specialist knowledge and skills are imparted in a special IE qualification program and put into practice in workshops, training courses and projects.

Close and trustful cooperation between persons responsible for BPS and IE in plants is an important factor in successful implementation of the BPS. Early involvement of IE experts in the planning process to design work systems, processes and products is the key to preventive work organization in order to avoid costly c orrective measures.

The IE experts use the Bosch Standard software CAPP- Knowledge® for this purpose, which g uarantees consistent time management from determination via preparation and documentation to the application of the time data.

True North

Using the BPS vision, we define where we want to go. In doing so, we use “True North” as a point of reference, as it characterizes the ideal state of a waste-free order fulfillment process.

At Bosch, we define the True North for the order fulfillment process as follows:

100% added value

Adding value without waste
- The aim is to optimally coordinate all activities that are required to add value, and to either avoid or optimize any superfluous activities that do not add value or do not increase the value of the product in the eye of the customer. The reference point is 100% added value. 100% delivery performance

Nothing will be delivered too early or too late
- the aim is to deliver the right product in the right quantity, at the right time and of the right quality with regard to the order data of the customer.

---

Zero defects

Avoiding defects rather than controlling them
- preventing defects from occurring from the beginning by introducing measures and making provisions for careful defect detection and prevention. Therefore the use of faulty components from an earlier work station must be prevented, as well as the passing on of faulty components to the next processing stage. A defect that occurs must be seen as an opportunity to completely and finally rectify the cause in the work cycle. It is important to learn from defects and understand them.

One-piece flow

Parts flow straight from one value-adding process to the next value-adding process and then on to the customer
- without any waiting periods or batch processing between these processes. There are therefore no buffer stocks between the processes.

It is useful to describe this ideal state
- True North
- as well as to subsequently arrange improvements and measure the progress made towards achieving this ideal state. True North is used as a point of reference, and we are trying to get as close to it as possible.

They often conceal the real problems in the value-added process, such as machine breakdowns or a lack of manufacturing quality, as they are used as a buffer. Reducing the stocks uncovers the problems and the causes can be eliminated. Consequences: stocks result in costs due to capital commitment and storage.

Furthermore, unlike a direct flow from process to process (one-piece flow ), material must be moved additionally, which causes extra handling. In addition, there is the risk that the stored parts will lose value over a period of time, become unusable (aging, corrosion), or will no longer be usable due to a technical change.

3. Waste caused by space:

Over-production, layouts of production equipment that are too large or are not oriented to the production flow increase the amount of space required.

Consequences: areas with dimensions that are too large cause long and nonvalue-adding routes, thereby increasing the time required. In addition, surface and space costs are also incurred.

4. Waste caused by unnecessary

movements: Unnecessary movements occur when, for example, associates have to walk around due to an inconvenient, non-ergonomic arrangement of tools or workpieces, have to find missing items, or cover long distances to collect materials. Consequences: these movements result in longer processes and waiting times, leading to long lead times (throughput times) and poor productivity.

---

5. Waste caused by transport:

It is often necessary to move materials and products from one place to another.

Transport only changes the position, but not the condition, of the product and, therefore will not contribute to the valueadded process.

Consequences: transportation ties up resources, leads to waiting times and results in costs for transport vehicles and transport systems. Transport damage can also occur.

6. Waste caused by waiting times:

Waiting times are time periods without activity by associates or machines. They occur, for example, because of a lack of materials, system malfunctions or slow process times ; product storage times also contribute to this.

Consequences: waiting times lead to long throughput times and reduced productivity.

7. Waste caused by errors/rework:

If the quality of parts or products is defective and does not meet customer requirements, these must be repaired or reworked.

Consequences: errors and rework disturb the production flow, extend throughput times and increase manufacturing costs.

The result is a poor ability to deliver, damage to the company’s image and loss of confidence in the company among customers.

What is Leveling?

Leveling is a methodology used to achieve a balanced utilization of resources as well as a constant flow and rhythm along the entire value stream, even in the event of fluctuations in customer demand.

To do this we decouple the production orders from the actual customer orders.

We create a production program that is as uniform as possible in terms of quantity and mix for a defined period, known as the leveling period. The customer demand is decoupled by means of a defined stock as buffer. Defined means that we allow stock only as high as calculated in advance and in a defined place in the value stream with the only purpose to decouple the customer demand. This defined stock is also a form of waste and bears potential for improvement by reduction.

As an associate may perform several work steps in a lean line; these work steps can be distributed over different work stations within this line. A lean line therefore is usually U-shaped in order to ensure short routes in changing work stations.

What is Cyclical Material Supply?

Cyclical material supply is a method to provide the right components in the required quantity and quality, at the right time and in the right place. The parts and products are supplied and removed in a standardized manner: in a fixed rhythm, on a defined route and in the smallest possible units (milkrun) . The material is supplied at a defined handover point, ideally directly at the Point of Use . What is Shopfloor Management Cycle?

Shopfloor Management Cycle (SMC) is a standardized method for following and analyzing output and losses from manufacturing lines. The aim is to use the transparency gained to react to deviations quickly and directly with correction measures, and thereby increase output and improve delivery capability. SMC activities are always focused on the bottleneck and pacemaker process of a value stream.

SMC is an integral part of the Daily Management routine (→ “Daily Management routine”).

What do we expect from SMC?

By continuously tracking the (hourly) output and the resulting losses, we ensure that we have precise information about output losses (quality losses, performance losses, set-up losses, organizational and technical losses) regarding the type of loss, the location where the loss occurred and its duration or the number of items lost. We then use this information to analyze the cause of these losses and to track improvement measures to increase output.

If we are able to increase output, we may potentially avoid future investments.

How do we implement SMC?

Once the target conditions have been defined, their achievment is addressed in individual System CIP projects. The input variable for each System CIP project is the defined target condition with the Monitoring KPI, stability criterion and standard. The standard is now developed, implemented and validated.

In addition, the Improvement KPI must be set for the processes involved.

The System CIP project ends with the validation and implementation of the respective target conditions. In other words, we must introduce each standard and demonstrate that it can be implemented. Stabilization of the new standard does not yet have to be achieved at the level of System CIP.

We use the next level of the BPS System

Approach, the Point CIP level, to achieve stabilization.

Point CIP as part of the BPS System Approach

The aim of Point CIP is to achieve stable and sustainable standards, i.e. the intervention limits defined for the monitoring KPI must not be exceeded within a defined period of time.

To achieve this stability, we must find out why the intervention limits have been exceeded. To identify the causes of d eviations , we use Improvement

Key Performance Indicators (Improvement

KPI), as these can be measured directly at the process and are a direct reflection of improvements. We also use stability criteria for the Improvement KPI, which means we operate with tolerable fluctuations and the respective intervention limits.

---

In addition to the above-mentioned criteria for the determination of intervention limits, we should take into account on Point CIP level that the individual stabilities on process level add up to the stability of the entire value stream. The intervention limits on Point CIP level must therefore be derived from all requirements of the value stream.

The five elements of Point CIP

Structured

Communication

Quick Reaction Process Sustainable

System Confirmation Problem Solving

Standards

The following five elements provide a structured approach to stabilize standards by ensuring that problems are solved permanently and their causes are eliminated. Sustainable problem solving is started as soon as the intervention limits specified for the standard are exceeded.

Point CIP element 1: Standards

The input variable for Point CIP is the new standard that is adopted from System

CIP and that has already been checked for feasibility.

Point CIP element 2: Process Confirmation

The key element of Point CIP reveals deviations from the standard and is the source for improvements that must be made to achieve the defined target condition. The process confirmation is relevant for both associates and managers.

Managers are required to monitor whether work is being carried out according to the standard and recognize deviations,

---

question these deviations rigorously and work together with the associates to improve the situation. The associates directly involved in the process also have a duty to report any deviations that arise and, where applicable, take action themselves.

Point CIP Element 3: Quick Reaction System

This is a defined procedure for handling deviations from the standard. It defines how quickly a response to the deviation is required, who should respond and what this response should be. We illustrate this process in a flow chart, for example.

Point CIP Element 4: Structured Communication

This is the exchange of information between the parties involved (e.g. manager, shopfloor manager and supporting operating units). This brief exchange of information is structured and takes place regularly.

It is used to define what the response to deviations from the standard should be.

Point CIP Element 5: Sustainable Problem Solving

Sustainable problem solving means finding the cause of the deviation, analyzing it, deriving concrete measures from the analysis and implementing these measures. The effectiveness of these measures is examined and confirmed following successful implementation so that the measures can be integrated into new or adapted standards where necessary.

To ensure that problems are solved systematically, a problem-solving sheet should be used, which provides support by describing the problem and the subsequent cause analysis step-by-step. In doing so, methodical elements relating to limiting the problem (gathering facts), limiting the cause (Ishikawa diagram) and analyzing the cause (3 x 5 x why?) are used.

Improvable System as the Basis of the BPS System Approach

Rather than providing independent and individual solutions, the BPS System Approach places an emphasis on improving the entire system in question or the entire value stream. All processes and resources within this system or the entire value stream are interdependent. Knowledge of the cause-effect relationships of these processes and resources is therefore essential to improving the system in a comprehensive and targeted way.

Establishing these cause-effect relationships requires clarity in the system under consideration or the value stream as a whole. This means:

- Defined material and information flows

- Defined and applied standards

- Decoupling of processes

- Clearly defined roles and

responsibilities

---

Establishing this clarity creates the necessary transparency and reveals any deviations from a target condition.

Furthermore, this clarity also allows to clearly establish the relationship between the cause of a deviation and the effect of a measure aimed at eliminating it.

We call a system that meets these criteria an “Improvable System”. It has potential for improvement because a target condition exists, deviations from this condition are visible and can be dealt with and cause-effect relationships can be identified. An Improvable System forms the basis for applying the BPS System Approach. However, it may be necessary for this

Improvable System first to be established within the value stream if it is not yet in existence. In other words, clarity must first be established in the system.

This is done within the BPS System Approach by defining projects within System CIP on the basis of the selected focus topics and then stabilizing them with Point

CIP (e.g. introducing leveled pull control before stocks can be permanently reduced).

System CIP cycle

Cyclical repetition and continuous improvement of the process

Application of the BPS System Approach is repeated cyclically. We refer to this as a System CIP cycle. Before a new System

CIP cycle is started, the previous cycle must be analyzed in detail. We examine whether the stated target conditions have been achieved. If there are any deviations from the target condition , the causes of these deviations must be identified.

Possible causes could be a process that has not been stabilized, an inaccurate description of the target condition, an unclear allocation of roles or an insufficient analysis of the current situation.

---

Questions from the BPS assessment may help during this analysis. The findings from the analysis are then incorporated into the new System CIP cycle.

The more frequently the System CIP cycle or BPS System Approach is completed, the more stable and rapid the improvement process is. Short repeat cycles and small incremental improvements enable us to respond more quickly and flexibly to changes in the framework conditions.

The first System CIP cycles generally take longer than later cycles. The more mature the organization is, the shorter the System

CIP cycles can be. Application of the BPS

System Approach is therefore also based on the principle of continuous improvement.

The content and procedure for the BPS system approach are described in the N62MBPS001 standard.

This form of organization is particularly beneficial in relation to process orientation, transparency and higher levels of personal responsibility.

5.5 BPS and Connected Industry

The history of lean production shows that adaptation to changes in framework conditions is frequently required. We are currently seeing trends toward different communication behavior, but also towards different consumer behavior. The “Internet of Things” (IoT) is becoming increasingly important in our world and for our company. The resulting changes will have an effect on production at Bosch and therefore on our production system, BPS. Here, as has been the case in the past, it is a question of building on established principles and making appropriate changes and adjustments. Even if terms such as “revolution” are used, most changes only have a gradual effect. It is essential that we are prepared so that these developments do not surprise us but rather we are able to handle them proactively.

Industry 4.0 is an integral part of the IoT and represents the physical production world merging together with the virtual world of information technology. People, machines, objects and systems are networked via the Internet and exchange information dynamically and in real time.

In networked production systems, all instances of the value-added chain from the supplier to the customer are connected across corporate boundaries.

5.6 Technical IT Solutions

The aim of BPS is to improve all activities in a value stream
- the total of all valueadding and non-value-adding business processes
- and to minimize waste. In the order fulfillment process, this results in a need for fundamental changes to some of the conventional processes in production program planning, production control and the material flow tracking system. BPScompliant processes also place high demands on the support from the relevant IT tools. These processes are designed to allow standardized and efficient handling of cyclically recurring activities. At the same time, integration into the existing

IT structure (SAP) is absolutely necessary, as the processes require a high degree of interlinking with customers and suppliers, as well as with internal processes for the invoicing of services rendered.

---

A range of standard tools are available for these requirements. These are either incorporated into the leading SAP system, or are connected to this system via specified interfaces. For example, the Manufacturing Execution System (MES) controls the manufacturing process and forms an important basis for Industry 4.0 applications. The MES makes it possible to plan resources, as well as control and manage production in real time, and it also records and prepares the operating, processing and machine data. Furthermore, it creates a virtual model of the production processes, monitoring and visualizing their status and comparing it with the KPIs (Key

Performance Indicator). Important KPIs include throughput times, availability, machine utilization, waste/rework, etc.

The MES also ensures it is possible to trace a product, right down to the raw materials and semi-finished products used in the application area.

The planning processes for manufacturing control, creating a leveled production program (leveling pattern) and the BPS-compliant design of consumption-controlled loops (leveled/non-leveled) are supported by Advanced Planning Systems to a varying degree. In addition to the SAP® Add-Ons

SAP LMPC® (Lean Manufacturing
- Production & Control) and SAP APO® (Advanced Planning & Optimization), this also includes the Bosch internally developed module NIV+.

---

Depending on the system, in addition to the leveling function, other functions are also possible, including creating inflow-outflow diagrams and a surface to visualize the planning results, the inventory developments with defined reaction limits and the status control in production. The system is able to distinguish between runner and exotic part numbers using a Pareto analysis.

Furthermore, NIV+ makes numerous useful functions available for designing Kanban loops in a consumption-controlled system.

The aim of Advanced Planning Systems is to support the planner, not to handle planning processes in such a way that they are fully automatic. The planning results in the system are used as the basis for further processes, e.g. for procurement or planning preliminary processes.

In operation, various ALPE modules (Auto Logistic Production Execution) are available in the SAP system for controlling and tracking material flows.
